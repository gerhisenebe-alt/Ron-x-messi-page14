The Calm Before

Barcelona & Manchester — April 22, 2008

Ciutat Esportiva Joan Gamper — Barcelona 8:47 AM

The morning light came through the slats of the dressing room blinds in thin, pale strips, falling across the concrete floor like quiet little scars. Lionel Messi sat on the wooden bench in front of his locker — number nineteen — methodically winding tape around his left ankle. He did it the same way every time: two loops around the joint, one across the arch, a firm press with the thumb to seal it. Routine was a kind of armor. He'd learned that young.

The dressing room at La Masia's senior training facility was alive with noise. Carles Puyol was arguing with Víctor Valdés about something nobody else could follow — something about a restaurant in Sitges that may or may not have changed its chef. Xavi Hernández sat in the far corner with Andrés Iniesta, their heads tilted toward each other in that way they always did, speaking in the shorthand of men who had shared a pitch since adolescence. Deco was on his phone, laughing softly at something in Portuguese.

And then there was Ronaldinho.

The Brazilian appeared in the doorway with a towel draped over one shoulder and that grin — the one that could defuse a bomb — already half-formed on his face. But today there was something underneath it. Something watchful.

"Leo." His voice carried the warmth of a low fire. He crossed the room in three long, unhurried strides and dropped a banana and a protein bar on the bench beside Messi's knee. "You eat breakfast?"

"Sí, Dinho."

"You're lying." Ronaldinho lowered himself onto the bench beside him, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched. He smelled of aftershave and mint toothpaste. "Eat. You think I don't notice? I always notice."

Messi picked up the banana without protest. He'd learned that arguing with Ronaldinho about food was like arguing with the tide. The Brazilian had appointed himself guardian, chef, counselor, and father figure all in one — a role he took with a seriousness that surprised people who only knew his public persona. The dancing, the smiling, the samba — that was the show. Behind closed doors, Ronaldinho was something else entirely.

"Tonight," Ronaldinho said, his voice dropping a register, meant only for Messi. "Manchester United. You know who their star is."

"Cristiano Ronaldo," Messi said, peeling the banana.

"Yes. Cristiano." Ronaldinho said the name the way someone might handle a package they suspected was ticking. Carefully. "I've watched him. He's fast. Skillful. Very arrogant. But good — I won't take that from him."

"Better than you?"

Ronaldinho laughed — that deep, rolling laugh that could fill a stadium and make everyone in it feel like they were in on the joke. "Nobody is better than me, Leo. But that's not the point." He leaned forward, resting his elbows on his knees. "The point is — don't let him inside your head. He likes to do that. The stepovers, the stares, the little shows. It's all theatre. You? You just play. That's your gift. Don't let him turn it into a circus."

Messi nodded slowly, chewing. He'd seen the clips — the Portuguese winger with the oiled hair and the smirk that lived permanently on his face, the way he moved like every match was a audition for an audience that existed only in his own mind. He was everything Messi was not: tall, loud, deliberately spectacular. The contrast between them was almost absurd.

"He's also dangerous," Ronaldinho added, and his voice changed again — lower, harder, stripped of its usual playfulness.

Messi looked at him. "Dangerous how?"

Ronaldinho seemed to weigh his words, rolling them around in his mouth before letting them go. "There are rumors. Things people say about him that don't make it into the papers. His manager — Mendes — that man is very good at making problems... disappear." He shook his head slowly. "Just stay away from him off the pitch. ¿Entendido? This is a football match, not a dinner party."

"You're being dramatic."

"I'm being serious. There's a difference, and you should learn it." Ronaldinho stood, ruffling Messi's hair with a hand that was gentler than his tone. "Now finish that. We've got training."

Across the room, Deco had pocketed his phone and was watching the exchange with a crooked smirk. He nudged Xavi with his elbow.

"Ronaldinho is worse than a mother hen," he said, not bothering to keep his voice down. "Worse. At least a hen lets her chicks cross the road."

Xavi glanced over, unimpressed. "He's right to be careful. Leo is... you know how he is."

"Yeah, yeah. The prodigy. The jewel. The—"

"I'm serious, Deco. The kid's different. You saw what happened last month when Sylvinho told that joke in the bus."

Deco's smirk widened. "Oh, I saw it. I saw his whole face go red — like a tomato, Xavi. A full tomato. He looked like he was going to pass out. All Sylvinho said was that thing about the—"

"I know what Sylvinho said."

"And Leo just — froze. Stared at his boots like they'd suddenly become the most interesting thing in the world. I thought his ears were going to catch fire."

Xavi elbowed him sharply. "Don't. I mean it. If Ronaldinho hears you talking about it—"

"Hears me talking about what?"

They both turned. Ronaldinho was standing behind them, arms crossed, that grin gone — replaced by something flat and heavy. He placed one hand on Deco's shoulder, the other on Xavi's. The grip was friendly. The pressure was not.

"If either of you," Ronaldinho said, his voice barely above a murmur, "ever — and I mean ever — take Leo anywhere near a club, a bar with girls, a nightclub, a strip— anything that is not football and family, I will personally make sure you never walk onto a pitch again. ¿Me oísteis?"

Deco swallowed. The smirk was gone. "Entendido."

"Good." The grin returned like a light switching on. "Now let's go train. Big day tomorrow. Big day."

He clapped them both on the back hard enough to make Deco stumble forward a step, then jogged out toward the pitch, whistling a samba tune.

Deco looked at Xavi. "He's not joking."

"He never jokes about Leo."

Training that morning was sharp and physical. Frank Rijkaard, his patience visibly thinning as the season's pressure mounted, had them running pressing drills — high-intensity sequences designed to prepare for United's counterattack. The session was fast, aggressive, and occasionally brutal. Deco caught an elbow from Touré Yaya that left a red mark along his jaw. Puyol launched into a tackle on Gudjohnsen that was more war than football.

Messi moved through it all with that eerie quietness that had become his signature. The ball seemed to obey him without negotiation — glued to his left foot, shifting direction on a thought, accelerating past defenders who were still setting their stance. Even in training, even among teammates who witnessed it daily, there were moments of involuntary stillness. A collective held breath. The sound of people watching something they couldn't fully comprehend.

But Ronaldinho watched him with different eyes.

He saw what others didn't — the way Messi's gaze sometimes drifted during water breaks, the way he lingered at the edge of group huddles, listening but rarely speaking. The way he processed everything around him with a quiet, almost predatory stillness that seemed at odds with his gentle manner. This kid was not just talented. He was perceptive in ways that could get him hurt — the kind of person who absorbed the world too completely, felt it too deeply, and didn't always know how to protect himself from it.

During the break, Puyol jogged over, clapping Messi on the back with a hand that could palm a medicine ball.

"Ready for tomorrow, kid?"

"I think so."

"Think so? No. Know so." Puyol's eyes burned beneath the tangle of his hair. "This is Camp Nou. This is our house. And that pretty boy from Madeira? He's going to learn what it means to play here. We don't roll out carpets for anyone."

Messi smiled — small, barely there, like a crack in a wall. "He's good, Carles."

"He's a show pony. You're a footballer. There's a difference." Puyol paused, studying him. Then his voice dropped. "Also — Ronaldinho has been... extra. Lately. More than usual. Is everything okay with you?"

"He's just protective."

"Protective is one thing. This is..." Puyol searched for the word. "...obsessive. I saw him corner a journalist last month — a journalist, Leo — just for asking you a personal question. The man looked like he'd seen a ghost."

Messi's expression didn't shift, but something behind his eyes softened. He reached up and touched the spot beneath his left ear — a habit so ingrained he didn't even notice it anymore. The skin there was sensitive, almost inexplicably so, and pressing it gently had always calmed him since he was a child in Rosario, homesick and scared in a city that wasn't his.

"He's the closest thing I have to a brother," Messi said quietly. "Or maybe... I don't know. A father. The kind that actually shows up." He shrugged. "I don't mind."

Puyol watched him for a long moment, then nodded once. "Okay. But if it ever gets too much — if he ever crosses a line — you know where to find me."

The Marriott Hotel — Manchester 10:12 AM

Cristiano Ronaldo stood before the bathroom mirror in his hotel suite, adjusting his collar with the precision of a man who treated his appearance as a form of combat. His dark eyes studied their own reflection — critical, calculating, never quite satisfied. The morning light from the window caught the angles of his jaw, the sharp architecture of cheekbones that had already made him as recognizable in tabloids as on the back pages.

He looked good. He always looked good. That was part of the design.

"You're going to be late," said a voice from the bedroom. Jorge Mendes — agent, handler, architect of empires, keeper of doors — sat in the armchair by the window, scrolling through his BlackBerry with the speed of a man who treated information like currency. "The bus leaves in twenty minutes."

"I'm aware."

"Are you aware that there will be over two hundred accredited journalists at Camp Nou tomorrow? And that at least thirty of them will be looking for any reason — any reason at all — to write something that isn't about football?"

Cristiano's jaw tightened by a fraction. "Let them write."

Jorge looked up from the phone, and his expression shifted — from professional to something older, heavier. Parental, almost. He stood and crossed the room, stopping just behind Cristiano's shoulder. Their eyes met in the mirror.

"Cristiano. Listen to me." His voice was low, measured. "What happened in Lisbon — that stays buried. I have made sure of that. But it stays buried only if you are smart. Only if you do not give them a reason to dig. Do you understand the difference?"

The mention of Lisbon made something cold and heavy settle in the pit of Cristiano's stomach. He remembered the weight of it in his hand — the metal, the handle, the way the light had caught the blade. He remembered the sound. The sound was the worst part. He didn't remember much after the sound.

No. He wouldn't think about that. He couldn't.

"I said let them write," he repeated, but his voice had lost its armor.

Jorge placed a hand on his shoulder. The gesture might have looked tender to an outsider, but Cristiano could feel the control in it — the pressure of a man who owned a piece of him and knew it.

"I have worked very hard to make sure your history is clean," Jorge said. "Very hard. Don't undo that because you can't control your temper. Especially not in Spain. The Spanish press — they are not like the English. They are hungry. They dig. They enjoy ruins."

Cristiano turned from the mirror. "I know how to handle the press, Jorge."

"Do you? Because last month in Rome, you nearly put a photographer's camera through his face."

"He was in my face."

"And tomorrow there will be fifty more just like him. All in your face. All flashing. All shouting questions you don't want to answer." Jorge paused, letting the silence do its work. Then: "There's something else."

Cristiano raised an eyebrow. "What?"

"The boy. Messi."

"What about him?"

"Stay away from him. Off the pitch." Jorge returned to his chair, picking up the BlackBerry again as though the conversation were already over. "Ronaldinho guards him like a dragon guards gold. You breathe in the kid's direction outside of a football context, and you'll have the Brazilian press, the Catalan press, the Argentine press, and probably Interpol all over you."

Cristiano's lips curved into something that wasn't quite a smile. "Why would I breathe in his direction?"

"Because you breathe in everyone's direction. That is who you are. That is what I built you to be." Jorge didn't look up from the phone. "I'm serious, Cristiano. Messi is off-limits. The kid is... he's not like us. He's not hardened. Ronaldinho has made very sure of that."

Something flickered in Cristiano's eyes — curiosity, perhaps. Or the particular instinct of a man who had been told not to touch something and immediately wanted to know why.

"What do you mean, not hardened?"

Jorge finally looked up. "I mean he's innocent. Genuinely, almost impossibly innocent. He doesn't go out. He doesn't do nightlife. He doesn't do women, at least not publicly. Ronaldinho has built a wall around him — no parties, no clubs, no scenes. The kid trains, plays, and goes home. That's it. That's his entire life." He paused. "He blushes, Cristiano. When someone tells a crude joke — and footballers tell crude jokes every five minutes — his face turns red. Like a schoolboy. He's twenty years old and he blushes."

Cristiano stared at Jorge for a long, still moment. Then he laughed — a sound that was rich and warm and entirely at odds with the thing that lived behind his ribs, the thing that had a name and a face and a memory attached to it, the thing that lived in Lisbon and would never leave.

"That's... interesting," he said.

Jorge's eyes narrowed. "Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You said 'interesting.' I know what that means when it comes out of your mouth." Jorge stood, straightening his jacket. "Cristiano. I am telling you — stay away from Lionel Messi. He is not one of your projects. He is not a challenge. He is a kid who has no idea what people like us are capable of, and it would be best for everyone if it stayed that way."

Cristiano turned back to the mirror, adjusting his collar one final time. His reflection stared back at him — polished, beautiful, dangerous in ways that couldn't be seen in glass.

"Let's go," he said flatly. "We have a plane to catch."

The sun was sinking behind the Collserola ridge, bleeding amber and deep violet across the Barcelona skyline. Messi sat on the narrow balcony of his apartment in Pedralbes, his knees drawn to his chest, watching the city breathe below him. The air smelled of jasmine and exhaust — a combination that had become so familiar it no longer registered as two separate things. It was just the smell of home. His second home. The one he'd chosen, or that had chosen him.

His phone buzzed on the concrete beside him. He glanced down.

Dinho: Rest. Tomorrow is important. Don't stay up late. And EAT something real — not just cereal. I mean it. I will check your fridge.

He smiled despite himself and typed back with one thumb.

Leo: I'm not a child, Dinho.

Dinho: You are MY child. Now eat. And sleep. I'll pick you up at 8 tomorrow.

He put the phone down and leaned back against the railing, tilting his head up. The first stars were beginning to push through the haze — faint, uncertain, as if they weren't sure they were allowed.

Tomorrow, Camp Nou would be full. Ninety-eight thousand people, all watching. All expecting. The weight of that number sat on his chest the way it always did — not crushing, but present. A constant, gentle pressure that had become so much a part of him he sometimes forgot it was there.

And somewhere across the city, in some hotel room with crisp white sheets and a view he'd never see, Cristiano Ronaldo would be preparing too. He'd seen the interviews — the confidence that bordered on delusion, the way the Portuguese spoke about himself as though he were narrating his own legend in real time. It was impressive. It was also exhausting. Football wasn't a monologue. It was a conversation. And Messi preferred to speak quietly, to let the ball do the talking, to answer questions not with words but with direction and timing and the particular silence that comes when something is done so well it doesn't need commentary.

His phone buzzed again — the group chat, which he'd muted weeks ago but still checked out of a vague sense of obligation.

A tingle at the base of his skull, like the charged air before a storm. Like something was coming toward him, and he couldn't tell yet whether it was a gift or a catastrophe.

His mother used to kiss him there when he couldn't sleep. Now he pressed it himself, gently, rhythmically, the way you'd press a bruise to check if it still hurt.

Tomorrow, he thought. Tomorrow, everything changes.

He just didn't know how much.

He watched the goal against Real Madrid — the one at the Bernabéu, where Messi had shouldered past Sergio Ramos with a quiet, almost terrifying determination. Not a stepover in sight. Not a trick. Just forward, forward, forward, until the ball was in the net and the Bernabéu was silent.

"What," Cristiano answered.

"Are you watching Messi videos?"

Cristiano blinked. "How did you—"

"Because I know you. I've known you since you were seventeen. Stop it. Turn the phone off. Sleep."

"Goodnight, Jorge."

"Cristiano, I swear to—"

Cristiano closed his eyes.

He didn't know that tomorrow, on a pitch that had been sacred ground for a hundred years, he would stand across from a boy who was everything he was not — quiet where he was loud, humble where he was proud, gentle where he was sharp. He didn't know that the boy's eyes would find his across the tunnel and hold there for a beat too long, a second that would feel like a door opening. He didn't know about the sensitive spot beneath the boy's ear, or the way he blushed, or the dragon who guarded him with a ferocity that bordered on the pathological.

He didn't know about Lisbon, how it would follow him. He didn't know about the knife, how it never really left his hand.

He didn't know any of it yet.

But he would. Oh, he would.

Hotel Arts — Barcelona 10:47 PM

Gerard Piqué was in the hotel lobby, nursing a glass of sparkling water and scrolling through his phone with the casual boredom of a man who had done this a hundred times before. The way Ronaldo's eyes would track certain players with an intensity that had nothing to do with football. They'd exchanged numbers months ago, after a friendly between Argentina and Spain. Piqué had made a point of being warm with him — he recognized something familiar in Messi, something that reminded him of himself at that age. Young, talented, and utterly unprepared for the machinery that was about to swallow him whole.

He typed a reply, then waited. The response came quickly. Piqué's thumb hovered over the keys. He knew what Messi was really asking — not about tactics or formations, but about the weight. The pressure. The thing that sat on your chest at 2 AM and whispered all the ways you could fail.

He thought about Ronaldinho's warnings. The boy was sheltered — deliberately, protectively, obsessively sheltered. Ronaldinho had built walls around him, and those walls were both a fortress and a cage. Piqué understood that feeling. He'd been there himself, once. Young, talented, and desperate to make sense of a world that made no sense.

When Messi asked about Ronaldo, Piqué felt a cold knot tighten in his stomach. How could he explain Cristiano Ronaldo to someone like Messi? How could he convey the intensity, the hunger, the way Ronaldo could make you feel like the only person in the room and then forget your name five minutes later?

He typed carefully, choosing each word with the precision of a man who knew that some truths were too heavy to speak aloud. The phone buzzed again. Messi was pressing. Piqué leaned back in his chair, running a hand through his hair. He thought about the stories he'd heard. The whispered rumors. The way Ronaldo's eyes would follow certain players around the training ground, the way he'd find reasons to be near them. The way some of those players would suddenly become distant, closed off, as if they'd seen something they couldn't unsee.

Everyone knew Ronaldo was basically both a womanizer and a manizer. It was an open secret in the football world — whispered in corridors, joked about in private, never spoken aloud in public. The Portuguese star had appetites that were legendary, and he pursued them with the same relentless drive he brought to the pitch.

Piqué had seen it firsthand. The way Ronaldo's gaze would linger on certain teammates. The way he'd find excuses to touch them — a hand on the shoulder, a pat on the back that went on a beat too long. The way he'd invite them to his hotel room after a match, just to "talk." The way some of them would emerge looking different. Haunted. Or worse — hungry.

But Piqué had never been one of those teammates. He'd always known why. Too tall. Too confident. Too Catalan. Ronaldo liked a certain type — smaller, quieter, easier to dominate. The kind of person who would be flattered by his attention, not suspicious of it. The kind who would think it was an honor to be chosen, not a warning sign.

Messi was exactly Ronaldo's type. And Piqué hated that he knew that.

He sent his final reply, then pocketed the phone. The chat was over, but the unease lingered. He thought about the way Messi had asked about Ronaldo — not with fear, but with curiosity. Like he was trying to solve a puzzle. Like he wanted to understand something that couldn't be understood from the outside.

And what he'd learned about Cristiano Ronaldo was simple: the man was a force of nature. A hurricane in a tailored suit. He didn't take what he wanted — he absorbed it. He consumed it. He made it part of himself and left nothing behind but the memory of his presence.

Piqué just hoped Messi would never find out what that felt like.
